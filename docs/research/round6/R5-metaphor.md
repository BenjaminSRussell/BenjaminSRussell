# R5. When a visual metaphor helps and when it is decoration

Round 6 research, 9 Oct 2026. The question: when does a borrowed form (a map, a chart, a desktop, a picture of a
thing) help a reader understand a subject, and when is it only decoration? What does the research say, and what
test follows for deciding whether a map form is earned by its subject? Then: what does that mean for the first
image on Ben's profile?

The test throughout is the owner's goal: every map, and every element in it, has a purpose that comes from the
projects themselves, not a reason found afterwards ("chasing a purpose instead of having a purpose").

"Read" means I opened the document and worked from its text (quotes below are from that text). "Abstract" means I
worked from the abstract, a publisher or catalogue record, or a summary by a named author. Where a source was only
confirmed as existing, it says "catalogue". The shared web-search budget for this turn ran out after about forty
searches in this report, so a few items stay at abstract level; nothing here is invented to fill a gap.

## Findings

### F1. The "useful junk" studies do not defend decoration. They defend pictures that carry the data or the message [4, 3, 10, 11, 12]

Bateman et al. compared 14 charts by Nigel Holmes with plain versions holding the same data, with 20 participants
[4]. Reading accuracy was no worse for the embellished charts, and recall after two to three weeks was better. The
authors' own explanation is the important part: the images "are actually tightly connected to the subject and even
the details of the chart ... the shape of the mouth and teeth show the increasing trend of the bars" [4]. The monster
is the trend. Few, re-analysing their data, lists what the winning charts had in common: one simple message stated in
the title, a few values, "the embellishment is tied metaphorically to the message of the chart", and a skilled artist
[3]. His rule: "nothing that supports the chart's message in a meaningful way is junk", and "embellishments that
represent data, even metaphorically, can themselves qualify as data ink" [3].

The later studies split the same way. Haroz et al.: "superfluous images can distract", but pictographs that represent
the data carry "no user costs" [10]. Skau et al.: most decorative changes to bar shapes made comparisons less
accurate [11]. Borgo et al.: embellishment helped memory and slowed visual search [12]. So the dividing line is not
"plain versus decorated". It is "does the picture encode something true about the subject, or not".

### F2. Being memorable is not the same as being understood [6, 7, 8, 9, 28]

Borkin et al. scored 2,070 visualizations for memorability; pictures of recognizable things, colour and unusual forms
made them more memorable [6, 7]. Borkin herself: "A memorable visualization is not necessarily a good visualization"
[7]. The follow-up found that what people recall is carried mostly by the title and text, and that pictograms help
recognition only when they are used appropriately [8]. Kosara, defending the work, agrees that memorability and
comprehension are "separate issues" [9]. Blackwell's experiments on metaphor in diagrams found the benefit of
metaphor is "largely restricted to mnemonic assistance" [28]. A theme can make an image stick. It cannot by itself
make it teach.

### F3. A good metaphor maps relations, not looks. A match on appearance alone explains nothing [23, 30, 27, 22]

Gentner's structure-mapping theory: in an analogy, "relations between objects, rather than attributes of objects,
are mapped from base to target", and the relations chosen are the ones that form a connected system [23]. A match
on looks only ("a sunflower looks like the sun") is a "mere appearance match"; such matches "can be appealing and
locally useful, [but] their explanatory power is sharply limited" [23]. Hudson applies this to interfaces: "the
greatest benefit to be gained from metaphor in UI design is from conceptual structure, not superficial appearance",
and "metaphors that offer attractive graphics at the expense of a useful structure ... are counterproductive" [30].
Cooper goes further and calls metaphor-led design "one of the most insidious of the many myths" in software [27].
Lakoff's Invariance Principle adds a constraint from the target side: the borrowed structure must be consistent with
the real structure of the thing it describes [22].

Applied: a week of commits drawn as an island borrows the look of land (a blob with a coast). None of the relations
that make land matter on a chart (you can reach it or not, it is a hazard or a harbour, the water between is the
route, depth tells you where you can go) has a counterpart in "commits in a week". It is a mere appearance match.

### F4. A metaphor always makes claims of its own. Readers will draw them whether or not the designer meant them [21, 45, 35, 36, 38, 20]

Lakoff and Johnson: "The very systematicity that allows us to comprehend one aspect of a concept in terms of another
... will necessarily hide other aspects of the concept" [21]. A metaphor brings its whole system of inferences.
Ziemkiewicz and Kosara found that the metaphor of a visualization (containment in a treemap, levels in a node-link
tree) changes how people answer questions about the same data [45]. Montello, Fabrikant et al. state a "first law of
cognitive geography": "people believe closer things are more similar", and grouping into a cluster makes people infer
a category [35, 36]. Skupin and Fabrikant report that people read clusters as "concentration of related documents"
and that this did not depend on their background or training [38]. Tversky: when a graphic shows something
non-spatial, "distance in space conveys distance on some abstract dimension" [20].

So a drawing of islands is not neutral styling. It tells the visitor: these are places; the water between them is a
gap or a passage; things near each other are related; a bigger island is a bigger thing. The owner's question "is it
based on the size of the project or how many commits?" is this inference at work: the form told him size means
something, and the image could not say what.

### F5. A graphic must encode all the facts and only the facts [16, 42]

Mackinlay's expressiveness criterion: a set of facts is expressible in a graphical language if a sentence of it
"(1) encodes all the facts in the set, (2) encodes only the facts in the set" [16]. A design that implies extra,
false facts fails even when the intended facts are all there. Lo et al., coding more than a thousand reported
misleading charts into 74 issue types, name two that fit decoration directly: "Not Data" (drawn to illustrate a
concept, but readers take it as drawn from data) and "Non Sequitur" (readers expect information from it, but it
holds none; "mostly created for decorative purposes") [42].

Applied: the coastline wiggle, the two-tint shallows and the island outlines encode no fact about the projects; the
build script says the shallows are only "the exact 2-D buffer of the land" [48]. By [16] they are ink that implies
facts (depth, approach, hazard) that are not there.

### F6. Area is a weak way to show an amount; position along a common scale is the strongest [17, 15, 42, 11]

Cleveland and McGill ranked judgements: position on a common scale is most accurate, then length, then angle and
slope, and area near the bottom [17]. Bertin gives position (the two planar variables) all four properties, ordered and
quantitative included [15]. Lo et al. note that, by Stevens' power law, people underestimate amounts shown as area
[42]. The current islands put the count of commit-days in a week into the thickness of a blob, read as area [48], so
even the one number they carry is shown on one of the weakest channels.

### F7. The form should match the structure of the subject. Space for space, line for time, path for flow [20, 19, 18]

Tversky's Congruence Principle: "the structure and content of the external representation should correspond to the
desired structure and content of the internal representation" [20]. Graphics that show real space have "a clear and
obvious advantage" because they "use space to convey space"; everything else depends on a spatial metaphor that has
to be chosen to fit [20]. Zacks and Tversky show that the form alone changes the reading: people describe bar charts
as discrete comparisons and line charts as trends [19]. Munzner's nested model names the failure upstream of drawing:
"wrong abstraction: you're showing them the wrong thing", with the example of hyperlink-graph views for web search,
where "people do not need an internal mental representation of this extremely complex structure", so every encoding
"incurred additional cognitive load for the user rather than reducing it" [18]. Many designers, she writes, "assume
the first abstraction that comes to mind is the correct one, and jump immediately into the third visual encoding
level" [18].

That is the owner's "chasing a purpose" in research terms: the form (a sea chart) was chosen first and a subject was
then found to fill it (weeks of commits), instead of the subject's own structure choosing the form.

### F8. In interface design, metaphor helps only where the actions carry over [24, 26, 28, 25, 31, 30]

The desktop worked because the objects behaved as office objects do: documents, folders and file drawers you open,
file and move, with the user's conceptual model designed before any code [24]. Carroll, Mack and Kellogg tabulated
where a metaphor holds and where it breaks (editor as typewriter: typing carries over, a block move does not) and
argued the mismatches must be designed for [26]. Hudson's counter-example is the Lotus Organizer, which copied the
rings and tabs of a paper diary without its useful structure [30]. Halasz and Moran's title, "Analogy Considered
Harmful", states the old warning [25]; Kuhn and Frank tried to formalize which parts of a spatial metaphor carry over
[31]. Blackwell, testing metaphor directly, found "surprisingly little benefit" for reasoning tasks [28]. A borrowed
form earns its place when what a person can do with the source (open, file, follow a route) is what they can do with
the target.

### F9. The skeuomorphism research is about signals, not ornament [32, 33, 34]

The studies that favour realistic design measure whether people can find things and tell what is clickable:
Burmistrov et al. found flat icons took about twice as long to find as realistic ones, and clickable objects on flat
pages took longer to identify, with more errors [32]; Nielsen Norman Group found weak signifiers cost users more
effort [33]. Apple's 2013 move to flat design was justified as "deference": the interface should help with the content
and never compete with it [34]. Both sides agree on the rule: a realistic or borrowed look is justified when it tells
the reader what something is or what to do with it, and not otherwise.

### F10. Maps of non-spatial things work when the space is computed from a relation that matters, and lose to a table for lookups [40, 38, 35, 37]

CodeCity draws a program as a city: buildings are classes, districts are packages (so nesting is real containment),
building height is the number of methods and base size the number of attributes [40]. With 41 participants it gave
more correct answers in less time than Eclipse with a spreadsheet [40]. But the same authors note that "while
CodeCity is faster at building an approximate overview of systems, a spreadsheet is faster at finding precise answers
in large data sets" [40]. Spatializations of documents work because distance is computed from similarity, which is
what readers assume it means [35, 37, 38]. In both cases the geography is derived from a real relation in the subject.
In the current image the only spatial relation is the calendar: horizontal position is the week, and vertical
position is a list order. A calendar has no geography to navigate.

### F11. Decoration does not buy trust, and the words around a chart steer what people take from it [44, 43, 41, 46, 47]

Dragicevic and Jansen tried four times to replicate a claim that adding a chart makes a statement more persuasive;
it did not (N = 623); the chart helped understanding very slightly and persuasion not at all [44]. Kong et al.: the
slant of a title changed the main message viewers took from the same chart, and viewers still judged the chart
neutral [43]. Pandey et al. show common distortions shift interpretation [41]. Monmonier: every map simplifies, so an
accurate map has to tell white lies, and the choice of which ones is the mapmaker's responsibility [46]. Harley: maps argue,
including through what they leave out [47]. The owner's standing rule (honest data only, every figure defined) is
what this research asks for; a chart look is read as measured fact, so every mark has to be one.

### F12. Effort and engagement help only when they lead into the data [14, 13]

Hullman et al. argue some visual difficulty can aid learning when it makes the reader process the data actively [14].
Andry et al. found embellishment, "within bounds", increased engagement with little cost [13]. Neither result covers
an element that leads nowhere: the engagement has to end at a fact about the subject.

### F13. The current image records its own reasons, and several are reasons about the theme [48, 50]

The build script states each element's rule and reason [48]. For the shallows the reason given is "land, shallows and
water read as a chart at a glance, without a word". That is a reason about the look of a chart, not about the
projects; by F1, F3 and F5 it is the definition of decoration. The light "Fl 30s" on Scrapy is the Prometheus scrape
interval of `scrapy_app` in `monitoring/prometheus.yml`; the Helm template sets the same job to 10 s and the global
default is 15 s [50]. It is a pun on "scrape" set in a lighthouse's notation: a match on a word and a look (F3), and a
figure that is one of three configured values. The "0.1.3 · PyPI" mark is a true and useful fact (version 0.1.3 in
`Cargo.toml` and `pyproject.toml`, installable from PyPI [49, 51]); its problem is placement on a commit timeline, not
content.

### F14. The projects do have structures a reader could follow [50, 51]

Scrapy's README describes a four-stage pipeline: Stage 1 discovery and URL extraction, Stage 2 content analysis and
validation, Stage 3 summarization and aggregation, Stage 4 enrichment, with Delta Lake storage [50]. rustmapper's
source is a route as well: seeders (`ct_log_seeder.rs`, `common_crawl_seeder.rs`, `sitemap_seeder.rs`) feed a

These are ordered paths with stops, hand-offs and failure points: relations, in Gentner's sense, that a route chart

exists in the subject.

## The test: when is a map form earned by its subject?

From F1–F12, a borrowed form is earned when all six answers are yes. Each question names its sources.

1. **Relation.** Can you name a relation in the source (a sea chart) that is also a relation in the projects, such as
   "a route has an order of stops", "a hazard sits at a place on the route", "what is connected can be reached"?
   A likeness of appearance does not count. [23, 30, 22, 24]
2. **Inference.** List what a reader will infer from the form (land is a thing, water is a gap, near means related,
   bigger means more, a line is a path). Is every one of these true of the projects? [21, 35, 36, 38, 45]
3. **Only the facts.** Does every mark encode a fact about the projects, and does no mark imply a fact that is false
   or absent? [16, 42]
4. **Channel.** Is the quantity that matters most on position or length, not area or texture? [17, 15, 42, 11]
5. **Faster than text.** Does a visitor get it faster from the picture than from one sentence of the README? If a list
   would do, it stays a list. [20, 18, 40]
6. **One message, carried by words and image together.** Is there one sentence the visitor should leave with, does
   the text around the image state it, and is the most visible element the subject of that sentence? [8, 43, 3]

## What this means for the owner's image and page

Each point can be checked against the image, and each ties to his goal: a purpose that comes from the projects, not
one found afterwards.

1. **Remove the islands and the shallows.** They fail tests 1, 2, 3 and 4 above (F3, F4, F5, F6). His own question
   ("is it based on the size of the project or how many commits?") is the evidence that they make a claim the image
   cannot back (F4). Check: no closed shape in the image whose outline is not computed from a fact about a project.

2. **Choose the map's subject before its look.** Write the one sentence the visitor should learn about his work
   (for example, how a URL travels through rustmapper, or how Scrapy's four stages hand data to each other) and only
   then choose the form that has the same structure (F7, Munzner's "wrong abstraction" [18]). Check: the build
   script's reason for the map names a project fact, never "reads as a chart".

3. **Allow a chart form only where the subject has a route.** Scrapy's Stages 1–4 and rustmapper's
   seeder → frontier/WAL → crawler → writer are ordered paths with stops and hand-offs (F14), which is the relation a
   route chart carries (F3, F10). Check: every stop drawn names a module, stage or store that exists (`grep` in the
   clones), and every line between two stops is a real hand-off of data.

4. **Write down the inferences and verify each one.** For any form kept, list what a stranger will read into it
   (near means related, bigger means more, a line is a way through, a mark is a place to watch) and make each one true
   or remove the cue (F4). Check: a reviewer can point at the code for every "near", "bigger" and "connected" in the
   picture.

5. **Every reason in the `BREAKS` table must name the projects.** Rewrite each rule's reason so that it says what a
   visitor learns about the work; any element whose only reason is how it looks ("read as a chart at a glance") goes
   (F1, F5, F13). Check: grep the table for reasons that mention land, chart, sea or water without a project fact.

6. **Keep the theme in the manner only where the manner carries a fact.** Typography, the neat line and a restrained
   palette can stay as style; a borrowed symbol (a light, a sounding, a coast) stays only if it encodes a true fact of
   the same kind it encodes on a real chart: a mark you steer by is a real failure point, a depth is a real limit
   (F1 via Few's "tied metaphorically to the message" [3], F9). Check: for each borrowed symbol, one sentence of the
   form "on a real chart this means X; here it means Y, and Y is the same kind of thing because ___".

7. **Drop "Fl 30s" or replace it with something a visitor can use.** It is a word match (scrape) in a lighthouse
   notation, and it picks one of three configured intervals (10, 15, 30 s) [50] (F3, F13). If an operating figure
   stays, it must be the one a visitor would act on, with the file it comes from.

8. **Put the most important number on position or length.** If a quantity is shown (workers, stages, records,
   versions), it sits on a common baseline or a measured line, never in the area of a blob (F6). Check: no figure is
   encoded by area.

9. **Let text do what text does faster.** The list of repositories with languages, and the "15 more", are lookups; a
   list beats a picture for those (F10: the spreadsheet beat CodeCity on precise answers [40]). Check: anything the
   README table already says is not drawn.

10. **Make the role line and the picture say the same thing.** People take their message from the title and the text
    and believe the chart is neutral (F2 [8], F11 [43]). The role line ("Crawl and data infrastructure · Python and
    Rust") should name what the picture shows, and the picture should show that and nothing else. Check: the subject of
    the role line is the most visible element of the image in a five-second look, at 870 px and at 390 px.

11. **Do not count on the theme to make it memorable or credible.** Memorability comes from distinct pictures of real
    things and from the title (F2), and a chart look adds no credibility (F11 [44]). The only theme worth its ink is
    one that teaches (F1). Check: remove each theme element in turn; if the visitor learns nothing less about the
    projects, it stays out.

12. **Run the six-question test at every review round.** It turns "is this chasing a purpose?" into checks a
    reviewer can answer yes or no for every element, with the table above as the format. An element with any "no" is
    redesigned or removed before the round closes.
