# R4. How people actually read a GitHub profile and a README

Round 6 research, 9 Oct 2026. Question: who lands on Ben's profile, what do they look at, for how long, on what screen, and what do they need from the first image and the page? Every implication at the end is tested against the owner's goal: each thing on the page must teach a visitor something true and useful about his actual projects, and must not be there to chase a theme.

Load-bearing claims come from primary sources: papers, GitHub's own docs, the study PDFs, the clones and PyPI. Career-blog numbers about "seconds on a GitHub profile" turned up often, but none had a stated method, so nothing below rests on them. No study measures time spent on GitHub profiles directly. The closest measured evidence is recruiter eye-tracking on resumes and profiles, web dwell-time studies, and interview studies of hiring on GitHub.

## Findings

F1. The decision is made in seconds, on a handful of facts. Recruiters spent about 6 s on the first fit/no-fit screen of a resume in 2012 and 7.4 s in 2018 [32, 33]. Almost 80 % of that time went to six data points: name, current title and company, previous title and company, and dates and education. "Beyond these six data points, recruiters did little more than scan for keywords", and detail "had little to no impact on the initial decision" [32]. A 2023 test that timed whole reviews got 17-46 s [34]. On the open web, leaving is most likely in the first 10 s and stays likely until about 30 s; pages that hold a reader past 30 s often keep them for 2 minutes or more [37, 38]. People form a reliable judgement of visual appeal from a page shown for 50 ms, and it colours what they think of the rest [44]. Nielsen's 1997 tests found 79 % of users scan and 16 % read word by word [35].

F2. A big picture takes attention away from the facts unless it is the facts. In TheLadders' study, recruiters on LinkedIn profiles spent 19 % of their time fixed on the profile photo "instead of examining other vital candidate information". A plainer layout with less visual complexity was "55 % easier to read" [32]. Users study images that carry information related to their task, and ignore decorative ones and generic stock photos [42]. Content that looks like an ad, or a large graphic that stands out on a phone, gets scrolled past even when it holds relevant content [43].

F3. Attention sits at the top and on the left. 57 % of viewing time falls above the fold, 74 % in the first two screenfuls and 81 % in the first three. The top 20 % of a page gets over 42 % [39]. Unstructured text gets read in an F-shape. The fix is to put the important points first and start headings and lines with the words that carry meaning: "if users see only the first 2 words, they should still get the gist" [36, 40].

F4. On GitHub, the README is the first thing on the profile that the owner controls. GitHub shows the profile README "at the top of your profile page" [2]. Under it come up to six pinned repositories and gists [3], then the contribution graph [2]. GitHub frames the profile README only as a way "to tell other people about yourself" [1]. Pinned cards (observed on github.com) show repo name, description, main language, stars and forks.

F5. Hiring readers want to see your own work and what others accepted, and they pick cues they can check quickly. In Marlow and Dabbish's interviews, employers went through public repositories and treated personal side projects as a signal of "love for programming". They treated commits accepted into well-known projects as a "community-level seal of approval". They distrusted popularity counts: watchers and followers depend on "how good a marketer that person was". Instead they looked at "the project where the applicant had made the most commits ... and then assess[ed] the actual code" [14]. The cues they valued were the ones "easy to verify quickly" [14]. A later study sums up the same work: most employers "focused only on the aggregate amounts of activity". When given a summary view, all nine participants used it to form a first impression and then drilled into detail [18]. Developers on GitHub size each other up from activity and project history [15, 16, 17]. Among HackerRank's hiring managers, a portfolio of projects or GitHub ranked second only to experience. Small companies weighted it more (80 % against 66 % at large companies), and executives ranked GitHub projects above years of experience [31].

F6. Activity counts are weak evidence, and GitHub itself retreated from them. In May 2016 GitHub removed the current and longest streak counters. Afterwards, long streaks collapsed: the share of 14-day streaks reaching 100 days fell from 4.4 % to 2.0 %. Weekend work and single-contribution days also fell. The authors conclude that the counters had steered people toward volume, not quality [20]. GitHub's achievement badges "hardly reflect their abilities", in developers' own words [19].

F7. The one activity fact that answers a real question is recency: is the project alive? Interviewees judged whether a project was "live" by its recent commits ("shows that the project is alive, that people are still adding code"), because "one does not want to adopt or contribute to a dead or dying project" [16]. A dated "last commit" or "CI passed" answers that question in one look. A week-by-week pattern adds little to it.

F8. A project README earns a user by answering what, why and how to start, in that order. GitHub lists what the project does, why it is useful, how to get started, where to get help, and who maintains it [4, 5]. In 4,226 README sections, "how" made up 58.4 % and "what" 16.7 %, but "why" only 2.7 %. Many READMEs "lack information regarding the purpose" [21]. The cognitive-funnel rule is to put the broadest information first, so a reader can leave as soon as the project is not for them [29]. Make a README asks for installation steps listed one by one, so that people get "to using your project as quickly as possible" [30]. Preston-Werner's case for writing the README first is that the README is the thing a user meets first [28].

F9. Wrong install instructions are the commonest documentation failure, and one that drives people away. Among 146 practitioners, "inappropriate installation instructions was the most frequently encountered issue", rated relevant by 65 % [25]. Developers rate ambiguity, incompleteness and incorrect content as the three severest problems, and six of the ten problems were "blockers" that made them switch to another API [26]. Newcomers who cannot find "the appropriate way to start" drop out [27].

F10. Popular repositories' READMEs are structured and carry images and links, but this is correlation, not cause. Contribution guidelines and references go with higher popularity [22]. READMEs that follow GitHub's guidelines and include install, usage, code snippets and images go with more stars [23]. In academic AI repositories, the strongest separators were links to other repositories, the number of images in the README, and a license [24].

F11. People cannot read a number from the size of a shape. Cleveland and McGill rank position on a common scale as the most accurate encoding, length next, and area near the bottom [49]. Heer and Bostock's crowdsourced replication found the same for circles and rectangles [50]. In recall tests, viewers spent the most time on text, especially the title. Titles that state the message improved what people remembered, and pictograms helped only "when employed appropriately", meaning tied to the data [48].

F12. People follow cues that promise what lies behind them [45]. A page should be self-evident, and where it cannot be, it should explain itself in a few words [46]. That is the owner's "if it looks like a shoe" rule, in usability terms.

F13. On GitHub, an image cannot be clicked into, and it may lose its dark-mode or width settings in the app. GitHub sanitizes README HTML, removing script, inline style, class and id [9], and serves images through its Camo proxy with its own cache [8]. An SVG drawn by `<img>` is an image: no script, no external resources [10], and no clickable parts of its own. The only link is around the whole image. `<picture>` with `prefers-color-scheme` has been supported on github.com since 2022, with `<img>` as the fallback [6, 7]. The GitHub mobile apps reportedly always report the light theme [13]. Staff said in 2021 that `width`/`height` on `<img>` were not supported on mobile [12]. GitHub's docs on the app say nothing about README rendering [11]. Both mobile reports are anecdotal, so they need a test on a real device.

F14. Phones are half the traffic, and phone readers read hard text more slowly. Mobile was about 50-53 % of web page views worldwide in mid-2026 [47]. On phones, comprehension matches desktop, but hard passages take longer (about 30 ms more per word), and "the need for brevity and prioritization is still critical" [41]. The owner names the iPhone as the common case (brief).

F15. Most decorated profile READMEs show exactly the signals hiring readers discount. The most-starred curated list's tool section is mostly stats cards, streak counters, trophies, visitor counters and typing banners [52]. The best-known card generator draws commits, stars and top languages [53]. Willison's widely copied profile README does something else: it lists his latest releases, blog posts and notes, the things he made, rebuilt every hour [54].

F16. A complex image needs a short alt text plus a long description in the page, giving the scales, values, relationships and trends [51].

F17. Project fact: the install block on the profile does not run as written. The profile README says `pip install rustmapper` and then `rustmapper crawl --start-url <your-site>` [57]. The published 0.1.3 wheel installs one command, `rust_sitemap`, and nothing named `rustmapper` [55]. The package declares no console script, and the Rust binary is named `rust_sitemap` [56]. rustmapper's own README says so, and asks readers to alias it [56]. A maintainer reviewer flagged the three names in an earlier round [57]. So the one line on the page meant to turn a visitor into a user fails with "command not found". That is the documentation failure developers meet most often [25].

## What this means for the owner's image and page

Each item is a check that can be run. All of them follow from one goal: each element must teach a visitor something true about Ben's projects, faster than the text would.

1. The first image will take a large share of a short visit, so it has to carry the main message about the work. It is the largest and first thing he controls on the profile [2, 4], and pictures absorb fixation time (19 % on LinkedIn photos) [32]. The island timeline spends that attention on when commits happened, which is the kind of aggregate count hiring readers discount [14, 20]. Test: show the image alone for 10 s to five people who do not know Ben, then ask "what does he build, and what is his main project?" It passes if at least four say crawlers or data infrastructure and name rustmapper or Scrapy. Ask also "what did the shapes mean?" If anyone answers with a guess about size or commits, the shapes fail. That was the owner's own reaction.

2. No shape size encodes a number. Area is among the encodings people read worst [49, 50], and the owner could not tell whether an island's size meant project size or commits. Test: every mark whose size, length or position stands for a quantity can be read without a legend, and the quantity is one a visitor cares about (F5, F7). Otherwise the mark goes.

3. Replace activity volume with the few facts hiring readers check. They look for: own projects and what they do; whether a project is alive; whether it is tested and released; and the project with the most work in it, so they can open its code [14, 16, 31]. Recency answers "alive?" [16]. Days-with-commits per week answers nothing they ask [20], and the owner already said "we don't need to show the commits". Test: every number in the image answers one of those questions, and its definition is in docs/data/AUDIT.md.

4. If the image is a chart, its subject has to be something a reader moves through, and the research points to the projects' own structure. Readers form an impression from an overview, then drill into the project with the most work [14, 18]. They follow cues that promise what is behind them [45], and they study images that carry task information [42]. For Ben the true "route" is how a URL goes through his crawlers, in rustmapper's words: seeds from sitemaps, CT logs and Common Crawl, a sharded frontier, the write-ahead log, redb, and the governor that slows the crawl when the database lags. That is a structure with an entrance and hazards. A weekly calendar is not. Test: each labelled part of the image names a real component that exists in the repo (a module or file a reader could open), and the text under the image links to it.

5. The words inside the image do the teaching, and the first two words of each line must carry meaning. Viewers fix on text and titles first, and titles that state the message drive recall [48]. People read the first words of lines [36]. Fine print such as "Datum: main" or "Eastern time" spends the scarce top-left on facts no visitor needs [39]. Test: read only the first two words of every text line in the image. A stranger should still learn what he builds and in what languages.

6. The phone edition must leave room for the facts in the first two screens. 74 % of viewing time is in the first two screenfuls [39]. Phones are about half of visits [47] and read hard material more slowly [41]. The current phone sheet is about 610 px tall at 390 px wide [57], close to a full iPhone screen. On a narrow screen GitHub stacks the profile header (avatar, name, bio) above the README, which pushes the image down further. Measure this on the device; it is not measured here. Test: on a 390 px viewport in Safari, the first two screens show his name, what he builds, his two main projects by name, and at least one link. Check it on a real iPhone, including the GitHub iOS app.

7. The light edition must stand on its own, because the apps may never request dark [13], and `<img>` is the fallback everywhere [7]. Test: open the profile in the GitHub iOS app with the phone in dark mode. The image that appears must be legible and must not be the dark edition clipped or scaled wrongly [12].

8. The image cannot link, so the first text line under it must name the same projects in the same order. Nothing inside an `<img>` SVG is clickable [10, 13]. Readers act on scent [45]. The current line "rustmapper · Scrapy · PyPI · Email" already does this [57]. Test: every project named in the image has a link in the first line of text below it, in the same order.

9. Fix the install block before anything else, because it is the one line meant to make a visitor a user, and it fails. `pip install rustmapper` installs `rust_sitemap`, not `rustmapper` [55, 56]. Wrong install instructions are the most frequent documentation failure and send people elsewhere [25, 26, 27]. Either show `rust_sitemap crawl --start-url <your-site>` (and the matching export line), or add `[project.scripts] rustmapper = "rustmapper:main"` (the `main()` wrapper already exists in python/rustmapper/__init__.py) and publish 0.1.4. Test: in a fresh virtualenv on macOS arm64 with CPython 3.13 (the only prebuilt wheel), paste the block exactly as shown on the profile. It runs without error.

10. Each featured project answers what, why and how to start, in that order, with the "why" kept [4, 5, 21, 29]. The rustmapper bullets already give the rare "why" ("the throttle watches the database, not the network ...") [57]. That is the strongest copy on the page, because it is the 2.7 % most READMEs skip [21]. Scrapy has what and why but no way to run it. Test: for each featured project, a stranger can say in one sentence each what it does, why it is built that way, and how to run it, using only the profile.

11. The pins must agree with the image and the text. Pinned repos sit right under the README, and readers open one or two of them [3, 14]. Test: the first two pins are Rust-sitemap and Scrapy, and each pin's one-line description matches the first sentence of its block on the profile. The three names (Rust-sitemap, rustmapper, rust_sitemap) also need one sentence that links them, or one name [57].

12. The alt text states the image's message, not its drawing. Today it describes commit weeks ("by week ... quiet Feb to Jul 2026") [57]. A screen reader, a broken image or a blocked proxy leaves the reader with only that [8, 51]. Test: read the alt text aloud on its own. It says what he builds and names the main projects, and any chart detail is described in the page text [51].

13. No element is there for decoration. Decorative images are ignored, and ad-like graphics are skipped on phones [42, 43]. The owner's rule is the same: "everything single thing inside of this needs a purpose". Test: for each element (the light "Fl 30s", the PyPI mark, the border, the shallows, the "15 more" row), write the one sentence a stranger learns from it without the theme being explained. If the sentence is empty, or needs the theme to make sense, cut the element. "15 more · 33" fails today. Hiring readers do look at side projects as a sign of interest [14], so either name what they are in a few words, or leave them to the repositories tab.

14. Do not copy the common profile kit. Stats cards, streaks, trophies and visitor counters are the most popular add-ons [52, 53], and they are the counts that researchers and GitHub itself found weak or harmful [19, 20]. Willison's model is the one that fits the goal: show the latest real things made [54]. Here that means the last release (0.1.3, 8 Nov 2025), the last CI pass and the last commit, each dated and each defined in the audit. Test: nothing on the page is a count of activity without a question it answers (F7).

Limits. No study measures how long people look at GitHub profiles. The seconds figures come from resume screens and general web pages [32, 33, 37], so they set an order of magnitude, not a budget. The README-popularity studies are correlational [22, 23, 24]. The mobile-app rendering reports [12, 13] are anecdotal and need the device test in item 7.
