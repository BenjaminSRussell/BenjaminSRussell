# R2: Pilot books, harbour guides, passage plans and logs: which ones get a stranger in

Round 6 research, 9 Oct 2026. The question: when a navigator has to enter a harbour they have never seen, what
do they actually read, what does it tell them, and in what order? Which of these documents help a stranger
arrive, and which are records that nobody reads to get anywhere? The answer is then held against the owner's
goal: every map and every element has to teach a visitor something true and useful about his projects, and
the picture should not be a chart just for the look of one.

Two primary sources were read in full text, not summarised from search results: a current NOAA Coast Pilot
chapter (source 3, dated 04 Oct 2026) and a real port pilotage plan (source 16). Counts quoted from them were
made on the extracted text.

## Findings

### F1. The documents a stranger reads exist because the chart cannot say what matters most [1, 2, 4, 8]

NOAA says the Coast Pilot "is intended to be used as a supplement to NOAA nautical charts. Much of the content
cannot be shown graphically on the charts and is not readily available elsewhere" [2]. NGA says the same of its
Sailing Directions (Enroute): they hold "additional information about coastal and port approach not depicted on
nautical charts, including winds, weather, tides, currents, ice, dangers, navigational aids, procedures,
regulations, and port facilities" [4]. UKHO sells its 76 volumes as "essential information to support port entry"
[8]. The chart shows where things are. Text tells you what they mean for you and what to do about them. Nobody
with a stake in getting a ship in relies on the drawing alone.

### F2. An approach is described in a fixed order: what the place is, what you see, the way in, the hazards, the help, where to lie [3, 5, 28, 31]

The Frenchman Bay entry in Coast Pilot 1 [3] runs in this order:

1. **What it is and who uses it**: "the approach to the towns and important summer resorts of Bar Harbor ...
   frequented by cruise ships, ferry vessels, fishing vessels, yachts". Size: "about 10 miles long".
2. **The verdict**: "Vessels of any size and draft can find anchorage. Navigation is not difficult for strangers."
3. **What you will see first, and from how far**: Cadillac Mountain, 1,530 ft, "visible from 35 to 45 miles
   seaward"; Schoodic Head, "the most prominent land feature at the eastern entrance"; a green tank "reported to
   be a good radar target".
4. **The way in**: "The principal entrance ... is from southward between Schoodic Peninsula and Baker Island, but
   small vessels can enter from southwestward through Western Way".
5. **Currents, ice, fog**: ebb "about 0.7 knot and sets southeastward"; fog "usually clears during the day".
6. **Pilotage**: who must take a pilot, where the pilot boards (to the second of latitude), and that you call one
   hour ahead.
7. **Where to lie**: "Winter Harbor is a good anchorage ... Bar Harbor ... has poor holding ground except near the
   head of the harbor."

Imray's *East Coast Pilot* does the same in a smaller space: "each estuary, river and port has its own chapter
which starts with the key information", then boxed waypoints and contacts, then the approach diagram [28]. The RYA
school's pilotage plan follows the same order for a yacht: sources, hazards for your boat, landmarks in order of
approach, tide at arrival, then "at least 2 options" for the night [31]. Bowditch's naval piloting preparation
asks the navigator to identify "a port's best NAVAIDS" from the Coast Pilot "if the piloting team has not visited
the port previously" [5]. The order is the order in which a stranger needs the facts.

### F3. The most frequent sentence in a pilot book is a plain judgement for the stranger [3]

In the one Coast Pilot chapter read [3], the words "stranger" or "local knowledge" appear 26 times. Examples, all
verbatim: "Navigation is not difficult for strangers." "Strangers can enter Dyer Bay with the aid of the chart in
clear weather in the daytime." "This channel should not be used without local knowledge." "Strangers should not
attempt passage with drafts greater than 4 or 5 feet and should go through on a rising tide." "Strangers should not
enter without assistance; local boatmen will act as guides if desired." The same chapter gives 20 ledges with the
height they uncover ("uncovers 9 feet") and uses "prominent" or "conspicuous" 47 times. Each sentence is a fact
plus what it means for a newcomer: can I do this alone, under what conditions, and what will I recognise. None of
them tells you how often the place was surveyed or when the surveyors were busy.

### F4. A real pilotage plan is one sheet, and every mark on it is something you act on [16, 6, 5]

The Port of Bundaberg pilotage plan [16] is a single A3 sheet: a chartlet of the reaches (Sea Reach, Middle Reach,
Inner Reach, swing basin, berths), and a table of course alterations. Each row names something you can see, the
new course and the distance: "Bcn No 20 transit with Bcn No 22, 218°T, 0.2'"; "Sea Reach Leads abeam, 229°T,
0.5'". It states one limiting number ("minimum UKC is 0.9m"), has a checklist (draft, tide, tugs, anchors ready)
and a line where the pilot and master sign that the plan "has been discussed with the Bridge Team". Nothing on it
is decoration. IMO A.893 sets out what such a plan must hold: the track with its true course, the dangers, the
minimum under-keel clearance in critical shallow areas, the wheel-over points, the method and frequency of
position fixing, and the contingency (where to go if the plan has to be abandoned) [6]. Bowditch adds danger
bearings, "hatched on the dangerous side" and labelled "NMT 074.0°T", so the navigator can tell at a glance which
side of a line is safe [5].

### F5. When the plan is missing, people run aground, and the cause is "no one could see the intention" [17, 18, 19]

The MAIB found that on CMA CGM *Vasco de Gama* the intended route "was not charted, and no decision points,
wheelover points, or abort options were identified", so "the bridge team could not monitor the pilot's actions"
[17]. On *Celtica Hav*, "a detailed pilotage plan had not been made by either the ship or the pilot" and the
exchange "did not cover all hazards" [18]. On *Surprise*, "the skipper had not assessed where safe and unsafe areas
existed" [19]. (Quotes for [18] and [19] are from the reports' published summaries.) What sank these passages was the lack of a shared picture of the route and the hazards. Lack of
data was not the problem: all three ships carried full logs.

### F6. The ship's log is a legal and administrative record. It is not written for the next ship, and no stranger reads it to get in [5, 14, 15]

Bowditch: "Deck Log: This log is the legal record of the passage. Record all ordered course and speed changes ...
Record who has the conn at all times" [5]. The UK Official Log Book schedule [14] requires musters, drills,
inspections, wage disputes, births, deaths, misconduct and draught. It does not require a course log at all. The
Danish order [15] says entries are for "shipowners, cargo owners, insurers or others having an interest in the
voyage" and must not be "erased, crossed out or otherwise made illegible". The log's readers are owners, insurers,
courts and inspectors, after the fact. A navigator approaching Bar Harbor does not open another ship's log. They
open the Coast Pilot.

### F7. Logs became useful to strangers only when someone boiled thousands of them down into directions [25, 26, 27]

Maury took the Navy's stored logbooks, "a gold mine of information" [26], reduced them to average winds and
currents by sea area and month, and published *Explanations and Sailing Directions to accompany the Wind and
Current Charts* (1851 to 1859) [25]. Ships using them averaged 144.5 days from New York to San Francisco against
187.5 for ships without them; one bark to Rio saved 17 days [25]. Old Weather volunteers transcribed about 1.6
million weather observations from Royal Navy logs for climate models [27]. In both cases the raw log is the input
and the useful thing is the summary: what the wind will do, where to go. Nobody improved a passage by looking at
when another ship's crew had been busy.

### F8. Sailing directions began as route descriptions, and charts were drawn from them, not the other way round [20, 21, 22, 24]

Medieval portolans were "constructed from the written information contained in contemporary written pilot
handbooks", and "the nautical chart never fully replaced the handbook, but remained supplementary to it" [20].
Garcie's *Grand Routier* (written 1483, printed 1520) gave courses, soundings, the nature of the sea bed and 59
woodcut coastal outlines that sailors "memorised before looking up at the coast in poor weather to find a match"
[22]. Waghenaer's *Spieghel der Zeevaerdt* (1584) was the first to put "the charts, coastal profiles and sailing
directions" in one volume [24]. Cotter traces modern pilot books, light lists and tide tables back to these [21].
Across five centuries the stable core is the route in words, plus a picture of what you will see. The map is the
supporting part.

### F9. The picture a stranger needs most is "what it will look like when I get there" [22, 23, 5, 28, 30]

The Admiralty kept coastal views "to provide navigators with depictions of the coast, especially harbours,
ports, navigational lights, markers and hazards", and later replaced the drawings with photographs [23]. Bowditch
tells a naval piloting team that the Sailing Directions "will have pictures of a port's NAVAIDS. This is especially
important for the piloting party that has never transited a particular port before" [5]. Imray pairs every
approach with "sea-level photography" showing what you should see from the water, plus aerial photos [28, 30].
Recognition depends on a true likeness of the arrival. An invented symbol does not do that job.

### F10. The small-boat plan carried on deck is a sketch, not a chart, and it drops everything you will not act on [31, 32, 33, 28]

An RYA instructor writes that the deck plan "doesn't have to be to scale, or use standard symbols, it just has to
have the information relevant to you", and warns that "the perfect plan is useless if it is only in your head"
[32]. The RYA school traces only the needed parts onto thin paper on a clipboard [31]. Practical Boat Owner
suggests marking clearing bearings "on a rough sketch of the channel so you don't need the chart on deck" [33].
Imray's best-known approach drawing, the "rolling road", is a straightened strip showing the buoyage sequence and
the track to steer through the hard part of an entrance [28]. Its geography is cut down to the order of the marks.
The deck version of a chart keeps the route, the marks in order and the hazards. Everything else goes.

### F11. Good guides say how sure they are, and they keep a date on every page [9, 10, 2, 3, 12, 13]

Charts carry zones of confidence. CATZOC runs from A1 (six stars) to D (two stars), with U for unassessed, so
mariners can "manage the level of risk when navigating in a particular area" [9]. The IHO warns that areas away from
shipping routes are often less well surveyed [10]. The Coast Pilot marks reported facts as "reported" (the tank
"reported to be a good radar target") [3], and a diagram that is "not drawn to scale ... should not be used for
navigation" [2]. Every Coast Pilot page carries its update date (04 OCT 2026 on the chapter read) [3]. Notices
to Mariners keep the books current, and mariners send corrections back on Hydrographic Notes [12, 13]. A guide's
worth depends on the reader knowing which facts are measured and how recent they are.

### F12. Guides are split by how far away the reader is: planning guide for the ocean, enroute for the coast, harbour guide for the berth [4, 2, 35, 36]

NGA keeps five Planning Guides, "relatively static", describing ocean basins and country-level facts (buoyage
system, time zone, pilotage rules). It keeps 37 Enroute volumes for coasts and port approaches, which are
"frequently updated" [4]. Coast Pilots go further in, because Sailing Directions "are intended exclusively for the
oceangoing mariner" [4]. Cruisers add Noonsite for the paperwork of arrival [35] and ActiveCaptain for crowd
reports on particular marinas and anchorages [36]. Detail goes where ships actually go. Waters nobody visits get
a line.

### F13. The structured future of sailing directions is "facts tied to a place", filtered to the voyage [11]

The 2024 IHO working-group paper proposes digital Sailing Directions as "geo-referenced feature objects"
(dangers, aids to navigation, pilotage, regulations, currents) so that mariners can "filter the required
information and get only what is necessary for a specific voyage" [11]. Even the people turning the books into
data keep the same unit: a fact tied to a place on the route, given to the reader who is going there.

### F14. People judging a developer's GitHub page use activity as a pointer to the work. The work is what they judge [37, 38, 39]

In Marlow and Dabbish's interviews, employers looked at "the project where the applicant had made the most commits
(as presumably that was the work they were the most serious about)" and then assessed "the actual code that was
written there" [37]. They rated project ownership and side projects as reliable signals. They rated watcher and
fork counts as unreliable [37]. Prana et al. found that READMEs commonly cover the "What" and "How" of a repository,
but "many README files lack information regarding the purpose and status of a repository" [38]. GitHub's own docs
say pins exist "so other people can quickly see your best work" [39]. Visitors are strangers looking for the way
in to the work, and the purpose and status of a project are the facts most often missing.

### Summary table: which forms help a stranger arrive

| Form | What it records | Who reads it, and when | Helps a stranger arrive? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coast Pilot / Sailing Directions (Enroute) | What a place is, what you see, the way in, hazards, pilotage, where to lie, and a verdict for strangers [2, 3, 4] | Navigator, before and during the approach | Yes. Written for exactly this |
| Harbour guide / pilot book (Imray, almanac) | Key facts box, waypoints, approach strip, sea-level photos, facilities [28, 29, 30] | Small-boat skipper, the evening before and on deck | Yes, most directly |
| Pilotage plan (port or yacht) | Route as a sequence of visible marks, courses, wheel-over, limiting depth, contingency [6, 16, 31] | Whole bridge team, briefed before entry | Yes. Its absence causes groundings [17–19] |
| Chart | Positions and depths, with zones of confidence [9, 10] | Everyone, all the time | Yes, but only with the text beside it [1, 2, 20] |
| Coastal views / photos | What the land looks like on arrival [22, 23] | Navigator at landfall | Yes, for recognition |
| Planning guide | Ocean-scale, country-level facts [4] | Navigator, weeks ahead | A little. Context, not the way in |
| Notices to Mariners / Hydrographic Notes | Corrections; new dangers reported back [12, 13] | Navigator keeping the books current | Indirectly. They keep the guides true |
| Deck log / official log | Who had the conn, orders, musters, births, deaths, discipline [5, 14, 15] | Owners, insurers, courts, inspectors, historians, after the fact | No. Useful only after thousands are boiled down into directions [25, 27] |

## What this means for the owner's image and page

He said: "If I was a sailor, if I was a pilot ... what I'd want are very helpful information. ... The map, very
helpful. The fucking log, not really." The record in F1 to F14 backs that up exactly. It also says why the
current first map fails: **it is a log drawn to look like a chart.** Each island is a week in which commits
happened, sized by how many days that week had a commit [44]. That is the form F6 describes: when the crew was
busy. It is the one form that does not help a stranger arrive. It has no route in, no marks, no hazards and no
berth, and it gives no verdict for the stranger. That is why the islands "teach you nothing": they encode the
calendar, and the calendar is not the project's geography.

Each implication below can be checked. Each test asks whether a visitor learns something true about his projects.

**I1. Remove the weeks-as-islands strip from the first image.** [F6, F7, F14]
Reason: it is a log, and a log does not help a stranger arrive. Even recruiters use commit activity only to find
which project to open [37]. Test: cover everything but the islands. If a visitor cannot say what any project does,
how to try it, or whether it works, the element goes. Today they cannot.

**I2. If activity appears at all, it becomes one boiled-down fact per project that tells the visitor where to
look, not 52 weeks of marks.** [F7, F14]
Maury turned thousands of logs into "go this way". The equivalent here is a single status per project, built from
fields the audit already defines [43]: "active, last commit 8 Oct 2026" or "dormant since Jan 2026". Test: every
activity mark on the page answers "should I open this one?" in one look, and its definition is in AUDIT.md.

**I3. Give each featured project the pilot book's order: what it is; the way in; what you will see; the hazard;
where to land.** [F2, F4]
All of these exist in the clones, so nothing is invented:
- *rustmapper* [41]. What: a concurrent Rust web crawler, also a Python package. Way in: `pip install rustmapper`.
  What you will see: `data/sitemap.jsonl`, then `sitemap.xml` (split above 50,000 URLs). Hazard, in the README's
  own words: the pip package is the Python library, and the command-line tool is the Rust binary `rust_sitemap`.
- *Scrapy pipeline* [40]. What: a multi-stage crawler (discovery, analysis, summaries, large documents) that writes
  to Delta Lake. Way in: `cd Scraping_project && python start.py`. What you will see: a Grafana dashboard at
  `localhost:3000`. Hazard, from its README: "From the clone root, `python start.py` fails."
Test: every line in a project's entry can be found in that repository at HEAD, and a stranger following only those
lines gets the thing running or reaches the right file.

**I4. Give each project a plain verdict for the stranger, like the Coast Pilot's 26 in one chapter.** [F3, F11]
Built only from measured fields [43]. For example: Scrapy has CI that passed on 8 Oct 2026 and 1,920 test
functions. rustmapper has CI that passed and 176 test functions. game_engine is dormant since Jan 2026, and its own
README says "1.5% verified completion rate". The verdict is the honest one-line equivalent of "not difficult for
strangers" or "not without local knowledge". Test: each verdict cites a field in `stats.json` and a definition in
AUDIT.md. No adjective without a measured fact behind it.

**I5. A map form is justified only where the project has a route a reader moves through. For a pipeline, the
right drawing is the rolling road: the stages in order, each a real place in the code.** [F4, F8, F10]
Scrapy's stages are real directories (`src/stage1/` to `src/stage4/`), and rustmapper's path is real (frontier,
fetch, redb with WAL, `sitemap.jsonl`/`sitemap.xml`, `classify`). A straightened strip of those marks is a true
geography: data travels along it, and a reader can navigate the code by it. Test: every mark on the strip names a
file, directory, command or output that exists in the clone. Delete any mark that does not.

**I6. Treat the picture as the deck sketch: keep only what the visitor will act on, with no symbol that needs
local knowledge to read.** [F10, F3]
"Fl 30s" is a true fact (Prometheus scrapes every 30 s), but only a sailor can decode it, and even a sailor learns
nothing about the crawler from it. The blue shallows and the island outlines carry no information. Test: a
visitor who has never sailed reads each mark correctly without a legend. Any mark that fails this goes, or is
replaced by the plain fact.

**I7. Where there is a picture of a project, show what the visitor will actually see when they arrive.** [F9]
Coastal views and sea-level photos exist so you can recognise the place. Real arrival views exist in the
repositories: a line of `sitemap.jsonl`, the Grafana dashboard, Wheel's `visual.png`. Test: every image of a
project is its real output or interface, not an illustration of it.

**I8. Spend detail where visitors go. Keep profile-level facts short, the way a planning guide does.** [F12, F14]
Time zone, languages, first commit and repository count are planning-guide facts. They change rarely and fit on
one line. The two or three projects a visitor will open get the enroute detail (I3, I4). The other repositories get
a list line each ("15 more" is fine if it links somewhere). Test: the first screen on a 390 px phone holds who he
is plus the way in to at least one project, and no row of marks about weeks.

**I9. Say how sure each figure is, and date the page once.** [F11]
The audit already sorts figures into measured and not worth showing [43]. Carry that onto the page: a measured
figure is printed plainly; a reported claim is marked as one, the way the Coast Pilot writes "reported". One date
stamp, equal to the stats build date, is justified, because CI status and last-commit facts change weekly, just as
Coast Pilot pages carry their update date. Test: no figure on the image lacks an AUDIT.md definition, and the
printed date equals `stats.json` `updated_at`.

**I10. The page succeeds when a stranger can brief themselves in under a minute. A plan only in Ben's head is
useless to them.** [F5, F4]
The groundings in [17–19] came from intentions nobody else could see. The README's job is to put the route in on
the page. Test, run with a reader who has never seen the profile: after 60 seconds on a phone, can they say (a)
what Ben builds, (b) which project to open first, (c) the command or link to try it, and (d) one honest limit? If
they can't, the page is not done, however good it looks.

**I11. The theme lives in the manner of the page and is never named.** [F2, F3, F10, standing rules in BRIEF]
What makes a pilot book feel like a pilot book is its manner: terse, ordered sentences, a judgement for the
stranger, marks in order, a dated page. It has no anchors, islands or compass roses. Writing the project entries
in that manner gives the "layer of a theme" he asked for, and no visitor has to be told it is a ship's anything.
Test: remove every nautical word and picture from the page. If the page still reads like directions for a
stranger, the theme is in the right place.
